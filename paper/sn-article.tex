\section{Methods}\label{methods}

\subsection{Network architecture}\label{sec:network_architecture}

The DG-CA3 network architecture, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:diagram-network}, was built primarily upon the models of
\citet{kopsickFormation2024, kimAdult2024, yangDynamic2025, chavlisDendrites2017} and reproduced at a scale of $\frac{1}{500}$ of
the rat hippocampus, the per-population cell counts being listed in Table~\ref{tab:neuron_counts}. Each neuron was reduced to a
single compartment, with no explicit dendrites or axons, yet kept biologically plausible: its neuron and synapse models, together
with the cell counts and connectivity, were adapted from Hippocampome.org, an open-access knowledge base that consolidates
multiple experimental sources on the hippocampal formation \citep{wheelerHippocampomeorg2023}.

The model included the core excitatory and inhibitory cell types of the DG-CA3 network underlying pattern separation and
completion. Although both regions contain many additional interneuron subtypes, several of which are also characterized in
Hippocampome.org \citep{wheelerHippocampomeorg2023}, this reduced set has been shown in previous computational models to be
sufficient to support pattern separation \citep{chavlisDendrites2017, kimEffect2024, yangDynamic2025}. The synapses between cell
types and their connection probabilities were likewise drawn from multiple sources and kept within biologically reasonable ranges.
Once all parameters had been gathered from Hippocampome.org and the remaining literature and the network had been assembled, a
subset of neuron and synapse parameters, as well as some connection probabilities, was tweaked within reasonable bounds so that
each cell type matched its \textit{in vivo} firing rate and population sparsity (Online Resource 1).

The network input is composed of cells from the entorhinal cortex, with a total of $N_{EC} = 400$
neurons~\citep{amaralChapter1990, kimAdult2024}. In each simulation, the EC as a whole presents a specific pattern, where each
pattern is represented by an active subpopulation of 10\% of EC neurons~\citep{mcnaughtonDead1991}. Inactive neurons not belonging
to the pattern do not spike during the simulation, whereas active neurons fire according to a Poisson process with at
$\SI{40}{\hertz}$. EC neurons project their axons via the perforant path to neurons with dendrites in the molecular layer of the
DG\@: GCs, MCs~\citep{scharfmanHilar2013} and BCs, as well as to CA3 neurons.

The GC population is split into mGCs and iGCs, the latter standing for the cells that adult neurogenesis produces during their
characteristic 4--7 weeks electrophysiological window \citep{aimoneRegulation2014}. Of the $N_{GC} = 2000$ GCs
\citep{westUnbiased1991}, 5\% are immature \citep{cameronAdult2001}, so that $N_{mGC} = 1900$ and $N_{iGC} = 100$. Respecting the
lamellar organization of the DG \citep{sloviterUpdating2012}, the GCs are distributed across 20 lamellae of 100 cells each. Every
GC contacts the BCs and CA3 neurons of its own lamella and, in addition, forms random non-lamellar excitatory connections onto
HIPPs and MCs.

iGCs possess distinct electrophysiological characteristics from the mGCs that render them more excitable than mGCs. Furthermore,
to simulate their still-incomplete circuit integration, iGCs receive EC afferent inputs at only a percentage $x_{EC \to iGC}$ of
the connection probability between the EC and mGCs, and also receive reduced inhibitory inputs from BCs and HIPPs
(Table~\ref{tab:synapse_params}). Additionally, some iGCs are known to also directly inhibit mGCs~\citep{lunaAdultborn2019}, so
the model includes an inhibitory synapse from iGCs onto mGCs.  Hippocampome.org~\citep{wheelerHippocampomeorg2023} does not
characterize iGCs. Therefore, the parameters used for iGCs were fitted from the spiking data of~\citet{espositoNeuronal2005} with
the simplex method of \citet{nelderSimplex1965} (Table~\ref{tab:izhikevich_neuron_params}).

BCs ($N_{BC} = 60$) are inhibitory cells that ensure sparse GC activation through winner-take-all competition among them
\citep{coultripCortical1992, chavlisDendrites2017, kimAdult2024}, where only the most active GCs in a lamella remain active,
inhibiting the rest. HIPPs ($N_{HIPP} = 60$) also contribute to the sparsity of GC activation, with a more global inhibition
acting on all GCs in a lamella. MCs ($N_{MC} = 100$) are excitatory hilar cells that receive excitation from GCs and project back
to GCs, BCs, HIPPs and other MCs via cross-lamellar connections. Although these excitatory MC to GC projections exist, their net
effect is generally inhibitory through the control of BCs and HIPPs~\citep{myersRole2009, scharfmanHilar2013}.

The HIPP parameters given by Hippocampome.org \citep{wheelerHippocampomeorg2023} reproduce a ``rebound burst spiking''
behavior, in which a burst of spikes follows the end of a hyperpolarization. Because large hyperpolarizations then drive
unbounded bursts that the network cannot tolerate, the HIPP parameters were instead taken from \citet{modakIzhikevich2018}.

CA3, unlike the DG, does not follow a lamellar structure in the model, as it forms a far more integrative neural
network~\citep{pakHippocampal2022, watsonHuman2025}. It holds $N_{pCA3} = 600$ pCA3 cells and $N_{iCA3} = 60$ iCA3 cells, the
latter modeled after CA3 basket cells~\citep{wheelerHippocampomeorg2023} so as to simplify the immense variety of inhibitory
neurons present in CA3~\citep{kopsickFormation2024}. Both neuronal populations receive afferent inputs from the EC and from GCs,
with iCA3 cells inhibiting pCA3 cells. The iCA3 cells inhibit the pCA3 cells, which in turn excite the iCA3 cells and, crucially,
wire recurrently among themselves, the substrate of the autoassociation and pattern-completion functions of CA3
\citep{kopsickFormation2024, rollsMechanisms2013}. A portion of the pCA3 cells further feed back to the DG by contacting MCs, a
projection that contributes to pattern separation \citep{myersPattern2011}.

All simulations were run in Brian2 \citep{stimbergBrian2019a} with the fourth-order Runge-Kutta method at a fixed time step of
\SI{0.1}{\milli\second} \citep{butcherHistory1996}; the neuron and synapse parameters appear in
Tables~\ref{tab:izhikevich_neuron_params} and~\ref{tab:synapse_params}. For each input pattern the network was left
\SI{300}{\milli\second} to reach a steady state, and its activity was then recorded over the following \SI{1000}{\milli\second}.
Synaptic connections remained fixed throughout.

\subsection{Neuron model}\label{sec:neuron_model}

Each neuron obeys the nine-parameter Izhikevich model \citep[chapter~8]{izhikevichDynamical2006}. This model was chosen in this work
and Hippocampome.org for being capable of reproducing the firing dynamics of neurons across a wide variety of conditions with a
biological plausibility approaching that of the Hodgkin-Huxley model \citep{hodgkinQuantitative1952b}, but at a far lower
computational cost. Its dynamics are described by:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq_izhikevich_1}
C_m \frac{dV_m}{dt} = k (V_m - V_r)(V_m - V_t) - u + I
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq_izhikevich_2}
\frac{du}{dt} = a [b(V_m-V_r) - u]
\end{equation}

where $V_m$ is the membrane potential, $u$ the recovery variable, $C_m$ the membrane capacitance, $V_r$ the resting potential,
$V_t$ the threshold potential, $I$ the total current into the neuron, and $k$, $a$ and $b$ constants that shape the neuron's
dynamics. These continuous equations are complemented by a discrete rule for action-potential generation
(Equation~\ref{eq_izhikevich_3}):

\begin{equation}
\label{eq_izhikevich_3}
\text{if } V_m \geq V_{\text{peak}}, \quad
\begin{cases}
V_m \gets V_{\min} \\
u \gets u + d
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

When $V_m$ reaches the peak value $V_{\text{peak}}$, a spike is emitted, $V_m$ is reset to the after-spike potential
$V_{\min}$, and the recovery variable is incremented by $d$, which makes the following spikes harder to elicit.

\subsection{Synapse model}\label{sec:synapse_model}

The synapse model follows the formulation of \citet{sennAlgorithm2001} and \citet{mongilloSynaptic2008a}. It captures short-term
depression, from neurotransmitter depletion, and facilitation, from calcium accumulation, acting over hundreds of milliseconds.
Five parameters describe each synapse (Table~\ref{tab:synapse_params}): the maximum conductance in the absence of resource
depletion $g$, the fraction of resources spent per spike $U_{se}$, the synaptic-current decay constant $\tau_d$, the facilitation
constant $\tau_f$ and the resource-recovery constant $\tau_r$ \citep{moradiNormalized2022}. The same table also reports the
connection probability $P$; although Hippocampome.org supplies these probabilities \textit{in vivo}, the reduced scale of the
network made it necessary to raise them so that activity could be sustained without losing sparseness.

Three state variables define the synapse: the utilization of synaptic resources ($U$), their recovery ($R$) and the fraction held
in the active state ($A$). All resources start available, so that $U_{t_0} = 0$, $R_{t_0} = 1$ and $A_{t_0} = 0$. They evolve
according to:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq_tsodyks_dU}
    \frac{dU}{dt} = \frac{-U}{\tau_f} + U_{se}(1-U_{-}) \delta(\Delta t_i)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq_tsodyks_dR}
    \frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{1-R-A}{\tau_r} - U_{+} R_{-} \delta(\Delta t_i)
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq_tsodyks_dA}
    \frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{-A}{\tau_d} + U_{+} R_{-} \delta(\Delta t_i)
\end{equation}

where $\delta$ is the Dirac delta, equal to 1 only when $\Delta t_i = t - t_i = 0$, that is, at the instant of a synaptic event
$t_i$; $U_{+}$ denotes the value of $U$ just after the event and $R_{-}$ the value of $R$ just before it. The resulting synaptic
current is:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq_tsodyks_I}
    I_{syn} = s \cdot A \cdot g \cdot (V_m - E)
\end{equation}

where $V_m$ is the postsynaptic membrane potential and $E$ the reversal potential, set to $E_{inh} = \SI{-86}{\milli\volt}$ for
inhibitory synapses and $E_{exc} = \SI{0}{\milli\volt}$ for excitatory ones. The constant $s = 10$, applied uniformly to every
synapse, is a scaling factor required by the reduced size of the network: with so few synapses relative to the rat hippocampus,
the network would otherwise fall silent. The synaptic currents $I_{syn}$ reaching a neuron are summed into the total current $I$
of its membrane equation (Equation~\ref{eq_izhikevich_1}).

\subsection{Pattern separation}\label{sec:pattern_separation}

Pattern separation was quantified following \citet{kimEffect2024}. To probe its dependence on input similarity and on adult
neurogenesis, 11 variants of the network were compared: a control without neurogenesis ($N_{mGC} = 2000$) and 10 with neurogenesis
($N_{mGC} = 1900$, $N_{iGC} = 100$) at increasing levels of EC$\to$iGC excitability, as illustrated in
Figure~\ref{fig:diagram-input-output}. This level, ranging from 10\% to 100\% in increments of 10\%, fixes the EC to iGC
connection probability as a percentage relative to that of the mGCs: with 100\% matching the mGC connection probability, and 10\%
meaning one tenth of that probability, and so represents the still-incomplete dendritic integration of the immature cells. Because
immature granule cells acquire their entorhinal input only gradually over the 4--7 week maturation window
\citep{espositoNeuronal2005, aimoneRegulation2014}, no single value of this excitability is representative of the immature
population; the full range was therefore swept, rather than assuming one level, with lower values corresponding to earlier, less
integrated cells and higher values to more mature ones approaching the connectivity of the mGCs. Each variant was presented with
30 sets of 10 input patterns, a set being one randomly generated reference pattern together with 9 derivatives of it graded from
90\% down to 10\% similarity. An input pattern is a binary vector of length $N_{EC} = 400$ encoding the state of each EC neuron,
its active neurons firing as Poisson processes at \SI{40}{\hertz} and exactly 10\% (40 neurons) being active in every pattern. A
pattern of similarity $X\%$ was obtained by keeping a random fraction of $X\%$ of the reference pattern's active neurons and
moving the remaining $(100 - X)\%$ to previously inactive neurons, which preserves the 10\% activity level.

Each pattern is simulated for \SI{1300}{\milli\second} of simulation time, with the first \SI{300}{\milli\second} discarded for
the network to stabilize. Separation was then read from the overlap between the activity patterns at the input (EC cells) and the
output (GCs or pCA3 cells). The output pattern is a binary vector of length $N$, the number of cells in the principal cell
population of an area, be it GCs or pCA3s, whose entries are 1 for neurons that fired at least once during the
\SI{1000}{\milli\second} window and 0 otherwise\footnote{Computing pattern separation from graded spike counts instead of binary
activation was also considered, but it yields nearly identical results (Pearson $r = 0.99$).}. For a pair of patterns $A^{(l)}$
and $B^{(l)}$, with $l \in \{in, out\}$ for input and output, the pattern distance $D_p^{(l)}$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:dp}
    D_p^{(l)} = \frac{O^{(l)}}{D_a^{(l)}}
\end{equation}

where $O^{(l)}$ is the orthogonalization degree  and $D_a^{(l)}$ the mean activation of the pair, itself the arithmetic mean of
the activation degrees of $A^{(l)}$ and $B^{(l)}$:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:da}
    D_a^{(l)} = \frac{D_a^{(A^{(l)})} + D_a^{(B^{(l)})}}{2}
\end{equation}

the activation degree of a pattern being its fraction of active neurons. The orthogonalization $O^{(l)}$, which gauges the
dissimilarity between the patterns, follows from the Pearson correlation coefficient $\rho^{(l)}$:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:o}
    O^{(l)} = \frac{1 - \rho^{(l)}}{2}
\end{equation}

Writing $\{a_i^{(l)}\}$ and $\{b_i^{(l)}\}$ ($i=1, \dots, N_l$) for the binary states of the $i$-th cell in $A^{(l)}$ and
$B^{(l)}$ ($l \in \{in, out\}$), this coefficient is:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:pearson}
    \rho^{(l)} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N_l} \Delta a_i^{(l)} \cdot \Delta b_i^{(l)}}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N_l} {(\Delta a_i^{(l)})}^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N_l} {(\Delta b_i^{(l)})}^2}}
\end{equation}

with $\Delta a_i^{(l)} = a_i^{(l)} - \langle a^{(l)} \rangle$, $\Delta b_i^{(l)} = b_i^{(l)} - \langle b^{(l)} \rangle$ and
$\langle \dots \rangle$ the mean over the whole population. Since $\rho^{(l)}$ ranges over $[-1, 1]$, the orthogonalization
$O^{(l)}$ ranges over $[0, 1]$ (Equation~\ref{eq:o}).

From the input ($D_p^{(in)}$) and output ($D_p^{(out)}$) distances, the degree of pattern separation $\mathcal{S}_D$ is taken as
their ratio:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:sd}
    \mathcal{S}_D = \frac{D_p^{(out)}}{D_p^{(in)}}
\end{equation}

A value $\mathcal{S}_D > 1$ means that the output patterns are more distinct than the inputs, that is, pattern separation, whereas
$\mathcal{S}_D < 1$ means the opposite, a convergence in which the outputs grow more alike.

\subsection{Population sparsity}\label{sec:population_sparsity}

Population sparsity measures how unevenly spiking is distributed across a population, being high when activity is concentrated in
a few cells and low when it is spread evenly. Following \citet{mchughAdultborn2022}, it was quantified with two complementary
indices computed on the per-cell spike counts accumulated over the recording window. For a population of $N$ cells with spike
count vector $x$ sorted in ascending order ($x_1 \leq \cdots \leq x_N$), the Gini index \citep{giniMeasurement1921} is:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:gini}
    S_{Gini} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N} (2i - N - 1)\, x_i}{N \sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i}
\end{equation}

where $i$ is the rank of each cell in the sorted vector. As a cross-validation, the Hoyer measure \citep{hoyerNonnegative2004} was
also computed:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:hoyer}
    S_{Hoyer} = \frac{\sqrt{N} - \left(\sum_{i=1}^{N} |x_i|\right) \big/ \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{N} x_i^2}}{\sqrt{N} - 1}
\end{equation}

Both indices range over $[0, 1]$, with larger values denoting sparser activity. They were computed for the mGC, iGC and pCA3
populations and for the combined granule-cell population (mGCs and iGCs together), and averaged over all patterns and trials, with
the standard error of the mean reported as the error.

\subsection{iGC activation and downstream circuit effects}\label{sec:igc_activation}

\citet{mchughAdultborn2022} showed \textit{in vivo} that briefly activating immature (4--7 weeks) adult-born GCs recruits
hippocampal interneurons and increases population sparsity in the DG and CA3. To test whether the model reproduces these circuit
effects, iGCs were activated directly through current injection and the response of every population was measured. The same
EC$\to$iGC excitability variants as in the pattern-separation experiment were used, each driven by an identical EC input.

In each run, a random half of the iGCs (50 of the 100) received a depolarizing current of \SI{0.5}{\nano\ampere} for
\SI{5}{\milli\second}, delivered \SI{400}{\milli\second} into the recording window, once network activity had stabilized. This
injected current $I_{ext}$ enters the neuron as part of the total current $I$ of the Izhikevich model (Equation~\ref{eq_izhikevich_1}):

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:i_ext}
    I = I_{ext} + I_{syn}
\end{equation}

where $I_{syn}$ is the summed synaptic current (Equation~\ref{eq_tsodyks_I}) and $I_{ext} = \SI{0.5}{\nano\ampere}$ during the pulse
and zero otherwise. For each variant the protocol was repeated over 30 trials of 10 input patterns, and the resulting 300 runs
were pooled for analysis.

The response of each population was quantified with a peri-stimulus time histogram (PSTH) of its firing rate, computed in
\SI{1.5}{\milli\second} bins over the interval from \SI{60}{\milli\second} before to \SI{60}{\milli\second} after the pulse. In
each bin the population firing rate is the pooled spike count divided by the number of cells, the number of runs and the bin
width, and is expressed as a z-score relative to a pre-pulse baseline, taken as the binned population rate from the start of the
recording window up to \SI{60}{\milli\second} before the pulse:

\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:psth_z}
    z(t) = \frac{r(t) - \mu_{base}}{\sigma_{base}}
\end{equation}

where $r(t)$ is the population rate in the bin at time $t$ relative to the pulse, and $\mu_{base}$ and $\sigma_{base}$ are the
mean and standard deviation of the baseline bins.

To relate the activation to sparsity, as \citet{mchughAdultborn2022} did \textit{in vivo}, the Gini index (Equation~\ref{eq:gini})
of each population was also compared, with a paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test over all runs, between the \SI{50}{\milli\second}
windows immediately before and after the pulse.

\subsection{Oscillation analysis}\label{sec:oscillation_analysis}

To characterize the rhythmicity evoked in BCs by the activation, the post-pulse population rate (\SIrange{5}{60}{\milli\second}
after pulse onset, in \SI{1}{\milli\second} bins) was detrended by subtracting a Gaussian-smoothed envelope ($\sigma =
\SI{8}{\milli\second}$), and the power spectrum of the residual was estimated by Welch's method \citep{welchUse1967}. The peak
frequency in the \SIrange{20}{200}{\hertz} band was taken as the dominant oscillation frequency (reported for BCs in Online
Resource 1).

\section{Results}\label{results}


% validation -> separation -> sparsity -> activation .

Before the effects of neurogenesis were analysed, it was first verified that the network settles into a stable and biologically
plausible behavior. Figure~\ref{fig:raster} shows the spiking activity of the EC, iGC, mGC and pCA3 populations during a
representative simulation. Driven by the EC input, the mGCs fire sparsely and in short, synchronized bouts, with only a small
fraction of the population active at any moment. The iGCs are more active, while the pCA3 cells fire at a moderate rate, spread
more evenly across the population.

This difference between the two populations can be seen in Figure~\ref{fig:firing_rate}: the iGC firing-rate distribution is shifted
towards higher rates than that of the mGCs, with a median firing rate of \SI{3}{\hertz} against \SI{1}{\hertz} for the mGCs. These
values closely match those found \textit{in vivo} by \citet{mchughAdultborn2022} (\SI{2.3}{\hertz} for adult-born against
\SI{1.0}{\hertz} for mature granule cells), although the shape of the distribution differs. For every cell type, the model's
firing rates and population sparsity fell within the ranges compiled from the literature (Online Resource 1), which is taken as
evidence that the network behaves plausibly.

\subsection{Adult neurogenesis enhances pattern separation in the DG and CA3}\label{sec:res_separation}

% [R] pattern separation (S_D) rises with neurogenesis/excitability, DG AND CA3,
%     Fig. results-1(b,d).  Stats: regression / Spearman vs excitability (see STATS note at end).

Figure~\ref{fig:dg-activity} shows that, with neurogenesis, the mGC population activation drops, and with each increasing level of
iGC excitability the mGC activation decreases further, from 7.9\% in the control network to 3.6\% at the highest excitability; the
opposite is true for the iGC population, since higher levels of excitability are expected to make the iGCs more active, with
almost 90\% of the iGC population being active for the highest level of excitability simulated. This happens because of the
feedforward inhibition exerted by iGCs upon mGCs, via iGC $\to$ mGC, iGC $\to$ BC/HIPP $\to$ mGC and iGC $\to$ MC $\to$ BC/HIPP
$\to$ mGC\@. For increasing levels of iGC excitability, as mGC activity decreases and iGC activity increases, an equilibrium of
approximately 8\% of the entire GC population (iGCs and mGCs counted together) stays active, similarly to the control model
without neurogenesis.

% TODO: better branching to CA3

This lower activation seen in the mGC population with adult neurogenesis directly affects pattern separation: with fewer neurons
active for a given pattern, the sparser coding leads to an increase in the mGC pattern separation degree, which rose
monotonically with iGC excitability from $\mathcal{S}_D = 2.40$ in the control network to $5.80$ at full excitability (Spearman
$\rho = 1.00$, $p < 10^{-6}$; Figure~\ref{fig:dg-separation}). The two granule-cell types, however, behave in opposite ways. Taken
on their own, the iGC outputs never separate their inputs ($\mathcal{S}_D < 1$, falling from $0.45$ to $0.20$ as excitability
rises), acting instead as pattern integrators, as also reported by~\citet{kimEffect2024}. When the whole granule-cell population
is considered together, pattern separation remains above one but decreases with iGC excitability (from $\mathcal{S}_D = 2.40$
to $1.22$; $\rho = -0.96$, $p < 10^{-5}$). Because the total granule-cell activation stays near 8\% across excitability levels,
this decline is driven almost entirely by a loss of output decorrelation, the mean pairwise correlation between output patterns
rising from $0.14$ to $0.55$, rather than by any change in activation. Since the role of iGCs is thought to be the modulation of
network activity rather than the encoding of information~\citep{mchughAdultborn2022, folszAdultborn2023}, the relevant quantity is
the separation achieved by the encoding population, the mGCs, which is enhanced by neurogenesis.

The same analysis applied to the pCA3 population (Figures~\ref{fig:ca3-activity} and~\ref{fig:ca3-separation}) shows that its mean
activation also decreases with neurogenesis and iGC excitability, from 29.7\% to 24.1\%, while its separation degree increases in
the same direction as that of the mGCs, from $\mathcal{S}_D = 0.51$ to $0.69$ ($\rho = +0.98$, $p < 10^{-5}$). Unlike the DG,
however, the pCA3 separation degree remains below one throughout: by the definition describe in Equation~\ref{eq:sd}, the CA3
operates in the convergence regime, its outputs more alike than its inputs. This is expected of a region whose recurrent
connectivity supports autoassociation and pattern completion~\citep{rollsMechanisms2013, kopsickFormation2024} rather than
separation. Neurogenesis therefore does not turn the CA3 into a separator, but reduces its convergence.

\subsection{Enhanced separation is driven mainly by increased sparsity}\label{sec:res_sparsity}

Figure~\ref{fig:sparsity_gini} shows that the sparsity indices of both the DG and the CA3 increase with neurogenesis and with the
progressive excitation of the iGCs, reproducing the central result of \citet{mchughAdultborn2022}, where adult-born granule cells
promote population sparsity throughout the hippocampus. Across the ten excitability levels, the Gini index rose monotonically for
the whole granule-cell population (from $0.93$ to $0.96$; Spearman $\rho = +0.99$, $p < 10^{-6}$) and for the pCA3 cells (from
$0.74$ to $0.78$; $\rho = +0.99$, $p < 10^{-6}$), each above its control value. The Gini index is shown here; the Hoyer measure,
computed for cross-validation, follows the same trend (Online Resource 1).

% TODO include in online resource the plot for orthogonalization

The separation degree depends on both the orthogonalization degree and the activation degree of the patterns
(Equations~\ref{eq:dp}--\ref{eq:sd}), so the mGCs could separate better by making their outputs sparser, more decorrelated, or both. Because the two combine multiplicatively, the change in $\log \mathcal{S}_D$ splits into an additive
contribution from each. Most of the increase (about 92\%) comes from the lower activation degree, as fewer mGCs fire for each pattern. The rest comes from
a smaller but real gain in orthogonalization, as the mGC outputs become less correlated; this is not merely an effect of the
sparser activity, since it remains when the number of active cells is held fixed (Online Resource 1). This extra decorrelation is
likely due to the stronger winner-take-all competition driven by the iGCs \citep{coultripCortical1992}.

\subsection{iGC activation recruits circuit-wide inhibition}\label{sec:res_activation}

Directly injecting current into half of the iGCs propagated through the entire DG--CA3 network, as summarized by the peri-stimulus
time histograms of Figure~\ref{fig:psth}, pooled over all EC$\to$iGC excitability variants.

Within the DG, the MCs were strongly excited and in turn drove strong excitation to the BCs. The BCs responded with a large,
significant increase in firing organized as a synchronous rhythm at $\sim\SI{123}{\hertz}$, within the range of maximum BC firing
rates reported \textit{in vivo}, which is up to $\sim\SI{230}{\hertz}$ \citep{lubkeSpecialized1998}.

The HIPPs, by contrast, were only weakly inhibited (peak $z \approx -1$), a counterintuitive outcome given that they receive
excitatory input from both the iGCs and the MCs; a plausible cause is that the transient drop in overall GC output following the
pulse withdraws the GC$\to$HIPP excitation that would otherwise engage them, outweighing the direct MC drive.

The mGCs displayed a biphasic response to the activation of the iGCs: a small excitation at first (peak $z \approx 4$), followed
by an even smaller inhibition afterwards, with the net effect over the \SI{60}{\milli\second} being excitatory. That neither the
direct iGC$\to$mGC inhibitory synapse nor the feedforward and feedback inhibition triggered by the pulse were able to suppress the
mGCs indicates that the model captures the iGC$\to$mGC interaction only imperfectly, and points to a missing inhibitory
contribution, plausibly from interneuron types not represented in the reduced circuit.

In CA3, the iCA3 interneurons were strongly and significantly activated, and this recruitment translated into a small inhibition
of the pCA3 cells through the iCA3$\to$pCA3 projection. \citet{mchughAdultborn2022} reported this interneuron recruitment to
follow iGC spikes with a lag of \SIrange{20}{40}{\milli\second} \textit{in vivo}; because axonal conduction delays were not
modeled, and all spike time were default to \SI{1}{\milli\second}, the absolute latencies here are shorter and not directly
comparable, although the temporal ordering of the responses is preserved.

Comparing the Gini index of each population between the \SI{50}{\milli\second} windows immediately before and after the pulse
confirmed that this recruitment of inhibition increased population sparsity in most of the models, the within-trial counterpart of
the steady-state sparsification reported in Section~\ref{sec:res_sparsity}; the full comparison across populations and
excitability levels is given in Online Resource 1.

Taken together, and in agreement with \citet{mchughAdultborn2022}, activating the iGCs recruited interneurons in both the DG and
CA3, providing the circuit mechanism for the rise in granule-cell population sparsity reported in Section~\ref{sec:res_sparsity}. The
model departs from their \textit{in vivo} findings in two respects: the sign of the mGC response, a net excitation rather than the
suppression expected of a principal cell, and the small inhibition of the pCA3 cells, for which \citet{mchughAdultborn2022} found
no effect. Their area-level recordings, however, could not separate the inhibitory contribution of the HIPPs from that of the BCs,
so the divergence between the two DG interneuron populations cannot be adjudicated against their data.
